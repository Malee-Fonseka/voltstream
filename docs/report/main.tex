\section{Use Case and Requirements}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Scope and Domain Problem}

This report documents \textbf{voltstream}, an end-to-end data platform for Use Case 3 of
the brief, \emph{Smart Grid Energy Monitoring and Billing}. It ingests continuous
smart-meter readings and a once-daily tariff file, and serves a live zone dashboard and a
daily per-household billing report. It is built on a \textbf{Lambda architecture}
(Section~\ref{sec:arch}). Every number in the Results section was measured on the running
system.

Supply must match demand continuously, and rooftop solar is generation the utility cannot
control: cloud over a zone can cut it within a minute, leaving a shortfall that slower
plant must cover. \emph{Renewable contribution by zone, right now} is therefore an
operational signal that is only useful if it arrives quickly. At the same time, the
billing department bills the same households from the same readings, combining block
tariffs, tier-dependent fixed charges, subsidy discounts and net metering of exported
solar.

\subsection{Interpreted Business Requirements}

The brief's business question has two halves, \emph{current grid load and renewable
contribution by zone} and \emph{each household's bill once the daily tariff is applied}.
They belong to two consumers with opposing requirements (Table~\ref{tab:twoconsumers}),
and most of our architecture decision follows from this table.

The phrase \emph{once daily tariff data is applied} means the bill depends on data that
does not exist until the day closes. That is a property of the business process, so no
streaming technique removes it. The brief's suggested outputs map onto delivered
components as follows: the real-time API is \texttt{GET /api/v1/zones/load} and the
dashboard's Live grid (Section~\ref{sec:res-live}); the low-renewable alert is the
\texttt{LowRenewableContribution} rule, plus four pipeline-health rules
(Section~\ref{sec:alerts}); the daily billing and solar report is served by the merge
endpoint, the dashboard and a Markdown report file per closed day
(Section~\ref{sec:res-report}).

\subsection{Functional and Non-Functional Requirements}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{F\arabic*.},leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item Ingest continuous meter readings, and one tariff file (plus a weather file) per
        simulated day.
  \item Retain every raw event immutably for reprocessing.
  \item Compute per-zone load and renewable contribution over event-time windows.
  \item Compute per-household daily bills with solar netting, block tariffs, fixed
        charges, subsidy and export credit.
  \item Serve a live dashboard, a daily report and threshold-based alerts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Simulated Clock}
\label{sec:simclock}

Time is compressed so a full day can be demonstrated in a session:
\textbf{1 simulated day = 5 real minutes} ($\mathrm{TIME\_SCALE} = 288$). Simulated time
starts at 2026-01-01 00:00 UTC when the stack starts, so day 1 is partial and 2026-01-02
is the first complete day. Each of 50 meters in 5 zones emits every 2 real seconds, 150
readings per simulated day, about 7{,}500 events per day in total. All windowing uses
simulated time; only latency metrics use the wall clock. A single module,
\texttt{simclock.py}, does the conversion, and every duration in
\texttt{config/base.yaml} carries a \texttt{\_sim\_minutes} or \texttt{\_real\_seconds}
suffix, so it is always clear which one is meant.

\section{Architecture Decision: Lambda versus Kappa}
\label{sec:arch}
% =====================================================================

\textbf{Lambda} \cite{marz} runs two paths over one immutable input: a batch layer that
recomputes exact results from all history, a speed layer that computes approximate results
from recent data, and a serving layer that merges them. \textbf{Kappa} \cite{kreps} runs a
single streaming path and corrects results by replaying the log through the same code.
\textbf{We chose Lambda.} Table~\ref{tab:archeval} evaluates both against the four
criteria in the brief.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\caption{Architecture evaluation. Replay and consistency decide it.}
\label{tab:archeval}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L{1.9cm} X L{1.9cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Criterion} & \textbf{Analysis} & \textbf{Verdict} \\
\midrule
Latency &
The consumers differ by about two orders of magnitude (seconds versus hours). One path
has to be tuned for one of them: long watermarks and full-day state make the operations
view late, while tuning for speed makes billing wrong. &
Lambda \\
\addlinespace
Replay &
Billing disputes and tariff corrections can arrive \emph{months} later, but Kafka
retention is \emph{days}. Kappa's correction mechanism, replay from an old offset, is
unavailable beyond retention. Lambda's batch layer replays from object storage, which
has no limit. &
Lambda \\
\addlinespace
Cost &
Lambda pays for two codepaths and a nightly compute burst. Kappa pays for long retention
or large long-lived per-household state, a cost that grows with households $\times$
horizon. Both are cheap at our volume. &
Lambda, narrowly \\
\addlinespace
Consistency &
Billing needs an immutable, auditable record independent of the message bus, with
lineage back to source readings. Lambda's master dataset provides it; in Kappa the only
ground truth is a log governed by a retention policy. &
Lambda, decisively \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\paragraph{The rejected alternative.} Kappa would also have worked here. Tariffs would be a
compacted Kafka topic keyed by household, one Structured Streaming job would join them to
readings, and corrections would replay from an earlier offset into a new table. Its
main advantages are one codebase (so the layers cannot diverge), one job to operate, and
one mental model. We rejected it for three reasons: (1) restatement must be possible months
later but retention is days, so its correction mechanism is not available when billing needs
it; (2) a finance audit trail should not depend on a message-bus retention setting; and
(3) holding tiered per-household billing state for months is more expensive and fragile
than a bounded nightly job.

\paragraph{Lambda's cost and how we contain it.} The same billing logic exists in two
codepaths and can silently diverge; for example, a speed layer ending the first tariff
block at 60\,kWh and a batch layer ending it at 61\,kWh would give different bills near
the boundary. To avoid this, all netting, tariff, rounding, key and
validation logic lives in one pure module, \texttt{voltstream/core/} (no I/O, 100\% test
coverage enforced in CI), imported by both jobs. The logic exists twice only where it must,
as scalar \texttt{Decimal} functions for the API and as Spark \texttt{Column} expressions for
the Spark jobs, and \texttt{tests/consistency/test\_pure\_vs\_spark.py} runs both on 5{,}000
random inputs plus sweeps of every block boundary and asserts identical output to the cent.
Divergence in logic is therefore a failing test; divergence in inputs (a stale tariff,
dropped late readings) is intended and is measured at run time
(Section~\ref{sec:res-recon}).

\paragraph{What would reverse the decision.} Lambda is justified only while the master
dataset must outlive Kafka's retention. Kafka tiered storage, or a table format such as
Apache Iceberg or Delta Lake, would give the log unbounded, queryable history; at that point
Kappa's single codebase becomes the better trade.

\paragraph{The three views.} The \emph{master dataset} is every raw event, unmodified, as
Parquet on object storage, partitioned by simulated date and hour. It is never updated in
place, and the archiver does no filtering, so any bug can be fixed by recomputing from it.
The \emph{speed view} holds 15-simulated-minute event-time windows per zone (consumption,
solar, renewable ratio, active meters) and a running provisional bill per household, priced
with \emph{yesterday's} tariff because today's does not exist yet. The \emph{batch view} is
built when a day's tariff file arrives: the whole day is read, validated, deduplicated,
joined with that day's tariff and billed, then reconciled against the speed view.

\paragraph{The merge function.} \texttt{GET /api/v1/households/\{id\}/bill?date=D} returns
the batch bill (\texttt{source="batch"}, \texttt{provisional=false}) if the run ledger
\texttt{pipeline\_runs} has a successful billing run for \texttt{D}, and otherwise the speed
estimate (\texttt{source="speed"}, \texttt{provisional=true}), as in
Figure~\ref{fig:merge}. The batch view always wins because it saw the late readings the
speed layer dropped and used the correct tariff. Every response says which view served it
and which day's tariff priced it.

\paragraph{Key transformations.} All windowing uses \textbf{event time} with a watermark,
because readings arrive out of order and arrival-time windows would put them in the wrong
window. \textbf{Solar netting} (\texttt{core/netting.py}) runs before tariff evaluation:
\begin{equation*}
  \mathit{self} = \min(\mathit{solar}, \mathit{consumption}),\quad
  \mathit{import} = \mathit{consumption} - \mathit{self},\quad
  \mathit{export} = \mathit{solar} - \mathit{self},
\end{equation*}
and $\mathit{total} = \mathit{energy\_charge} + \mathit{fixed\_charge} -
\mathit{subsidy} - \mathit{export\_credit}$, where the energy charge prices the import block
by block and money is rounded half-up to 2 decimals identically in both layers. Kafka is
at-least-once, so both layers \textbf{deduplicate} on $(\mathit{meter\_id},
\mathit{event\_ts})$, defined once in \texttt{core/keys.py}. The Kafka message key is
\texttt{household\_id}, which keeps each household's readings ordered within a partition.

\section{Technology Stack and Justification}
\label{sec:stack}
% =====================================================================

Each choice is justified against the use case's constraints, with the rejected alternative
(Table~\ref{tab:stack}).

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\caption{Technology selections and the constraint each satisfies.}
\label{tab:stack}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L{2.0cm} L{2.4cm} X L{2.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Selected} & \textbf{Constraint satisfied} & \textbf{Rejected} \\
\midrule
Ingestion & Apache Kafka 3 (KRaft) & A replayable log is a precondition for Lambda; independent
reads by two consumers give the two-branch fan-out & RabbitMQ; direct DB writes \\
\addlinespace
Stream \& batch & Spark 3.5 Structured Streaming and batch & One API for both layers enables
the shared \texttt{core/} module; native event-time watermarks & Storm; Flink \\
\addlinespace
Master dataset & Parquet on S3-compatible object store & Columnar, compressed, immutable;
partition-pruned full-day rescans; storage separate from compute & HDFS; raw CSV/JSON \\
\addlinespace
Serving store & PostgreSQL 16 & Joins, ACID writes for money, indexed point lookups; low
post-aggregation volume & Cassandra; MongoDB \\
\addlinespace
Orchestration & Apache Airflow 3 & Sensors, retries, run history and re-runnable per-date
billing runs (the restatement mechanism) & cron \\
\addlinespace
Observability & Prometheus, Alertmanager, Grafana & Pull scraping suits containers; alert
rules are version-controlled and unit-tested & Bespoke log parsing \\
\addlinespace
Serving API & FastAPI & Async, generated OpenAPI docs, schema validation at the boundary &
Flask \\
\addlinespace
Deployment & Docker Compose & One-command reproducible stack, no cloud account needed &
Kubernetes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Kafka.} Kafka is a log, not a queue: reading does not delete, so a speed-layer
bug can be fixed by resetting the offset, the producer is decoupled from consumer outages,
and the speed layer and archiver read the same topic independently. Spark tracks these
offsets in its own checkpoints rather than Kafka consumer groups; we confirmed that killing
one consumer left the other untouched. Three partitions bound parallelism, and the default
seven-day retention is the constraint behind Section~\ref{sec:arch}. RabbitMQ deletes on
acknowledgement, so it offers neither replay nor independent readers.

\paragraph{Spark.} Because Spark's batch and streaming APIs are the same, one billing module
serves both layers, which deals with Lambda's biggest risk directly. Spark
also supports event-time watermarks natively. Storm has lower latency but no event-time
windowing and no batch API; since we only need latency in seconds, that advantage did not help us.
Flink is a stronger pure streaming engine, but it lacks an equally simple shared batch path
in Python, and Spark already meets the latency target (6.3\,s against 60\,s).

\paragraph{Parquet on object storage.} Lambda needs all raw data kept: if a bill is wrong
because of a logic error (for example \texttt{>} instead of \texttt{>=} at a block
boundary), the fix is to correct the code and recompute from raw readings, which is only
possible if they were kept. Parquet gives column pruning, compression, real types and
predicate pushdown, and date/hour partitioning lets the daily job read only its own day. We
use a local S3-compatible store so graders need no cloud account; the code uses only the
S3 API, so moving to AWS S3 is a configuration change. Because MinIO stopped publishing
images, the stack runs \texttt{pgsty/silo}, a MinIO-compatible fork (decision D8). HDFS
was rejected as too heavy at this scale.

\paragraph{PostgreSQL.} Our reads are point lookups by household and date, recent-window
aggregations by zone, and joins between bills, the run ledger and households: small
relational results. PostgreSQL gives joins, transactions (a billing run lands completely or
not at all), \texttt{JSONB} for the block breakdown and idempotent upserts. Cassandra is
built for write volumes we do not have and cannot join.

\paragraph{Airflow.} Airflow schedules on real time, so ``once per simulated day'' cannot be
a cron expression. A \texttt{tariff\_watcher} DAG polls the landing zone every real minute
and triggers \texttt{daily\_billing} once per tariff file, with a run id taken from the file's
date (\texttt{billing\_\_2026-01-02}), so a repeat is skipped. A restatement is another run
for the same date (\texttt{billing\_\_2026-01-02\_\_r1}). DAGs only start Spark containers and verify
results; they contain no business logic. Cron has no dependencies, retries, history or
re-runs.

\paragraph{Prometheus, Alertmanager, Grafana.} Services expose \texttt{/metrics} and
Prometheus scrapes them; batch containers exit too early to be scraped, so they push to a
Pushgateway. Alert rules live in the repository and are unit-tested with
\texttt{promtool}, and Grafana dashboards are provisioned from files.

\section{Implementation}

\subsection{Data Sources and Ingestion}

Two Python simulators provide the sources. The \textbf{meter producer} computes each
household's load and solar for the current simulated time (solar is zero from 18:00 to
06:00), adds noise and publishes to Kafka topic \texttt{meter.readings} every 2 seconds:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={A meter reading event, keyed by \texttt{household\_id}.},label={lst:event}]
{"schema_version": "1.0", "event_id": "uuid4", "trace_id": "uuid4",
 "meter_id": "MTR-0042", "household_id": "HH-0042", "grid_zone": "ZONE-C",
 "event_ts": "2026-01-02T14:15:00Z",          // simulated time; drives all windowing
 "consumption_kwh": 0.412, "solar_generation_kwh": 0.180, "voltage": 232.4, "producer_id": "sim-01"}
\end{lstlisting}

The \textbf{reference dropper} writes one tariff CSV (per household: tier, subsidy flag,
three block rates, fixed charge, export rate, effective date) and one weather CSV per
simulated day into the object-storage landing zone. Tariff rates change by $\pm$0.50 per
day, so using yesterday's tariff has a visible effect. Both contracts are Pydantic models
exported as JSON Schema, and every tariff row is validated before use.

To test robustness, the producer deliberately injects faults at configurable rates
(Appendix A): duplicates, out-of-order events, null fields, negative kWh, unknown
households and meter dropouts that buffer readings and send them late. Invalid records go
to the dead-letter topic \texttt{meter.readings.dlq} and the \texttt{rejected\_records}
table with a reason. With all rates at zero the output equals the input, which a test
asserts.

\subsection{Processing}

\paragraph{Raw archiver.} A Spark stream that writes every Kafka record to Parquet,
unchanged, partitioned by simulated date and hour, and resumes from its checkpoint after a
restart. It guarantees \emph{no loss}, not exactly-once: Spark commits files and offsets in
two steps, so a crash between them re-reads a small range. We measured 334 duplicates out
of 14{,}012 rows after a kill, with no missing events; batch deduplication removes them.

\paragraph{Speed layer.} A second, independent Spark stream validates each record with
\texttt{core/validation.py}, deduplicates, groups readings into 15-simulated-minute
tumbling windows per zone under a 30-simulated-minute watermark, keeps a running total per
household, and upserts into PostgreSQL every 10 real seconds using \texttt{foreachBatch} in
update mode. The provisional bill applies \texttt{core/spark\_expr.py} with yesterday's
tariff. Two effects of time compression matter here. First, the 10-second trigger equals 48
\emph{simulated} minutes, longer than the watermark, so the trigger rather than the
watermark decides which late readings are dropped (measured in
Section~\ref{sec:res-recon}). Second, in update mode results are emitted every trigger, so
the watermark adds no latency; it only controls when old state is discarded.

\paragraph{Batch layer.} The \texttt{daily\_billing} DAG has eight tasks: wait for the
tariff; wait 90 real seconds for late data; run the Spark billing job (read the day's
Parquet, validate, deduplicate, join the tariff, apply the shared netting and tariff
functions, write bills and a \texttt{success} row in \texttt{pipeline\_runs}); check the row
count and that every bill satisfies the total formula; roll up exact daily zone totals and
check they equal the household totals; reconcile; and generate the daily report.

\paragraph{Reconciliation.} For each household the gap between the speed estimate and the
batch bill is split into a \emph{tariff effect} (the speed layer's kWh priced at today's
tariff, minus its estimate) and a \emph{data effect} (the batch bill minus that figure, i.e.\
readings the speed layer missed). The two always add up to the total gap.

\subsection{Code Quality and Testing}

The code is a typed Python 3.11 package organised by layer (\texttt{simulators},
\texttt{streaming}, \texttt{batch}, \texttt{storage}, \texttt{api}, shared \texttt{core} and
\texttt{contracts}). All settings live in \texttt{config/base.yaml}, validated at start-up.
There are 281 tests (unit, property-based, consistency and integration), \texttt{promtool}
tests for the alert rules, and a test that fails if alert thresholds or metric names drift
from the code. GitHub Actions CI runs linting, type checks, the tests with the 100\%
\texttt{core/} coverage gate and the alert checks on every push.

\paragraph{Structured logging.} Every service writes one JSON object per line with a fixed
envelope, so a record can be followed across containers with one filter:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={A speed-layer log line.},label={lst:log}]
{"ts": "2026-09-27T13:35:25.992Z", "level": "INFO", "service": "speed-layer", "stage": "speed-zone",

\texttt{service} and \texttt{stage} cover ingestion (\texttt{producer}), processing
(\texttt{speed-zone}, \texttt{archive}, \texttt{billing}) and serving (\texttt{api}).
Alertmanager sends notifications to an API webhook that logs them with \texttt{stage:
alert}, so alerts appear in the same log stream.

system watch for the main weakness of Lambda, the two layers drifting apart. The two \texttt{pg\_} gauges exist because the
renewable ratio is 0 every night (so a flat value cannot reveal a dead feed) and only the run

\paragraph{Tracing.} Full distributed tracing through Spark executors was out of scope, so

(PostgreSQL and object storage reachable) and \texttt{/api/v1/alerts/status}. Grafana has
three dashboards: pipeline health, grid operations and Lambda divergence.

\subsection{Resilience and Restatement}
\label{sec:res-resilience}
\label{sec:res-restate}

\paragraph{Crashing each path.} Killing the raw archiver (\texttt{SIGKILL}, 20\,s outage)
lost no events and left no offset gaps; the 334 duplicates were removed by batch
deduplication. Killing the speed layer for 60\,s made the live view 56\,s stale while the
archiver kept writing (1{,}526 readings); the speed layer caught up 22\,s after restart, the
previous day's final bill stayed at 237.38, and the day of the crash was billed in full.
So when the speed layer fails, the live view goes stale for a while, but no bill is affected.

\paragraph{Restating a day.} \texttt{voltstream.ps1 backfill} corrupted the 2026-01-02 tariff
(block 1 rate $+45.00$), re-billed the day, restored the file and re-billed again. The day's
total went from 18{,}737.41 to 44{,}876.57 and back to the cent. The ledger shows
\texttt{superseded}, \texttt{superseded}, \texttt{success}, each with its own Airflow run,
and \texttt{household\_bill\_history} keeps all three versions. Bills are recomputed from the
master dataset rather than edited in place.

\section{Discussion, Limitations and Production Scale}
\label{sec:limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Was Lambda the right choice?} We think so. The provisional bill had to be wrong (yesterday's
tariff), and the batch layer corrected it about 2.5 minutes after each day closed. The
restatement drill tested the main reason we rejected Kappa: recomputing a past
day from Parquet, independent of Kafka retention. Lambda's cost appeared as expected (two
jobs, an orchestrator, reconciliation), but the shared \texttt{core/} module kept all
divergence to inputs: no gap came from logic.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Time compression} speeds up event time but not processing time, so
        watermark tuning is not realistic; it is why the trigger outgrew the watermark and
        the live view misses about 1\% of energy. Raising the watermark above one trigger
        would fix it.
  \item \textbf{Simulation fidelity:} interval readings rather than cumulative meter
        registers; synthetic curves and tariffs; 50 households, far below a real utility.
        The weather file is produced but not used by billing or solar generation.
  \item \textbf{Small files:} one simulated day produced 245 Parquet files averaging
        10.2\,kB, far below Parquet's ideal size; production needs compaction or a table
        format.
  \item \textbf{Single points of failure:} one Kafka broker, one Spark node, one PostgreSQL
        instance; no schema registry; secrets are local defaults in \texttt{.env}.
  \item \textbf{Resources:} the stack needs about 9\,GB; on the demo laptop the divergence
        drill stalled on memory, so that alert was shown with a lowered threshold.
  \item \textbf{Monitoring gaps:} if \texttt{sql-exporter} stops, two alerts go quiet
        instead of firing; the Pushgateway keeps the last divergence value indefinitely.
        Tracing uses correlation IDs, not distributed spans.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{At production scale} we would use Iceberg or Delta Lake over raw Parquet
(transactions, compaction, time travel), a schema registry with Avro or Protobuf, change data
capture instead of daily tariff files, replicated Kafka and Spark on Kubernetes, watermarks
set from measured lateness, and alerts on exporter staleness. PostgreSQL would suit us up to
roughly millions of aggregate rows per day, after which a columnar store such as ClickHouse
fits better. Iceberg or Kafka tiered storage would also remove the retention constraint that
decided Section~\ref{sec:arch}, making Kappa the better choice.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

The same meter stream must give grid operators load and renewable share within seconds and
give billing an exact, auditable bill that cannot exist until the daily tariff arrives. We
chose Lambda because billing must be restatable after Kafka's retention has expired, and we
contained Lambda's main risk by sharing all billing logic through one tested module.
Voltstream implements this with Kafka, Spark, Parquet on object storage, PostgreSQL, Airflow
and FastAPI, reproducible with one command. On the running system the speed path delivered
zone metrics in about 6\,s, the batch layer finalised all 50 bills about 2.5 minutes after
each day closed, the merge switched from provisional to final, a corrupted day was restated
with its history kept, neither crash lost data, and all five alerts fired under their
faults. The main limitation is time compression, which distorts streaming behaviour; the
decision itself would change if the log became durable and queryable without limit.
